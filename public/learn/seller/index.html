Module 01

Is Your Project Actually Sellable? The AI Asset Score Check

"I built an AI tool. It has paying users. I think it's worth something. But I genuinely don't know if anyone would buy it, or for how much, or what I'd even need to do before listing it."

Most AI builders who want to sell their project think the main question is "how much." The real first question is "is it ready" — and the answer changes what you do next.

We score AI projects on 5 dimensions, each worth 0–15 points (with one risk criterion worth up to -10). Total 0–100. This is the AI Asset Score.

The fast self-check (do this now)

Answer these 7 questions honestly, score yourself 0–2 on each:

  • Revenue: Do I have at least 3 paying customers for 3+ consecutive months?
  • Autonomy: Has the product run without my direct involvement for at least 2 weeks?
  • Documentation: Is there a written handover document I could send someone today?
  • Access list: Can I list every account, key, and credential needed to run this?
  • Churn: Have I lost less than 5% of customers in the last 3 months?
  • Scalability: Could someone add 50% more customers without rewriting the core?
  • Transparency: Can a buyer verify everything I'm claiming?

10–14 out of 14: You're ready. Start Module 2.
6–9: Get a free AI Asset Score — you need specifics on what to fix.
Under 6: Focus on the product for 2-3 months, then come back.

Module takeaway

Sellability is about transferability, not just revenue. A project that makes $3k/month but only works because you're there every day is worth much less than one that makes $1k/month and runs itself. Fix transferability first — it's what unlocks the valuation premium.

Module 02

How to Price It Without Leaving Money on the Table

"Someone offered me $5,000 for my bot. It makes $600/month. I said yes without thinking. Later I found out I should have asked for $10,000–$15,000. I left half the value behind."

This story is common. AI builders systematically underprice their projects because they don't know the market benchmarks. Here's what the data actually shows.

The standard valuation formula

Valuation = Net Monthly Revenue × Multiple

Standard multiple for AI assets: 12×–24× monthly net revenue
$800/month net → fair range: $9,600 – $19,200
$2,000/month net → fair range: $24,000 – $48,000

Net revenue means after API costs, hosting, and any contractor costs. Not gross.

What moves you toward 24× (and above)

  • Churn below 2%/month
  • Revenue growing month-over-month for 4+ months
  • No single customer above 20% of MRR
  • Full documentation and clean codebase
  • No hard dependency on a single API provider
  • Proven operator fit (someone non-technical can run it)

What moves you toward 12× (and below)

  • High churn (5%+/month)
  • Flat or declining revenue trend
  • One customer is 40%+ of revenue
  • No documentation exists
  • Revenue tied to your personal brand or direct sales
  • Technical fragility (one API change kills it)

How to anchor your price correctly

Don't start with a number — start with the story. Present your financials as a timeline: "Here are the last 6 months of MRR. Here's the churn. Here's what it costs to run. At current numbers, you're looking at a 17-month payback, which is well inside the healthy 12-24 month range."

Then name your price. Anchored to data, not to what you feel you deserve or what you need to replace your income. Buyers pay for math, not for your sunk cost.

Common pricing mistakes
  • Pricing at "cost to rebuild" — buyers don't care what it cost you
  • Asking 36×+ MRR and defending it with "growth potential"
  • Undisclosing costs to make MRR look higher
  • Pricing based on one good month, not the 6-month average
  • Not having a written number ready — leads to lowball offers accepted under pressure
Module takeaway

Your price is Net MRR × (12–24). Where you land in that range depends on your churn, trend, concentration, and documentation quality. Know your number before you talk to any buyer — and know how to justify it with data, not feelings.

Module 03

4 Weeks to Transfer-Ready: Packaging Your Asset

"Everything is in my head. The code is a mess I never cleaned up. I have Notion docs half-started but they're outdated. I don't know where to begin."

Packaging is the single highest-ROI activity a seller can do. Proper documentation doesn't just make transfer possible — it raises your valuation by 30-60% and cuts time-to-close from months to weeks. Here's the 4-week plan.

  • Week 1

    The Credentials Audit

    List every single account, API key, domain, server, and service the product depends on. Organize into a spreadsheet: service name, URL, login method, current cost. This is your transfer manifest. If you can't complete it in a week, the product is not ready to sell.

  • Week 2

    The Technical Handover Doc

    Write a README-level document: how does this work? What does each part do? How do you restart it if it crashes? What are the main dependencies? Have a non-technical friend read it. If they can't follow the logic, it needs more work.

  • Week 3

    The Customer Map

    List every paying customer: name, email, plan, how long they've been paying, any notes about their usage or requests. Identify your top 3 by value. Note any at risk. This document is gold to a buyer — it shows you know your customers and the relationships can transfer.

  • Week 4

    The Operations Playbook

    How do you run this thing day-to-day? What do you check in the morning? What does support look like? What are the 10 most common customer questions and their answers? Write the operations manual an intelligent non-technical person can follow from day one.

✅ Transfer-ready checklist
  • All credentials documented and transferable
  • Technical overview written and reviewed by non-technical reader
  • Customer list with full data
  • 30-day operations playbook written
  • Last 6 months of financial statements
  • Known issues or risks documented honestly
  • Code repository accessible and readable

The last point matters: known issues documented honestly. Hiding problems doesn't work — buyers find them during due diligence or after close, and both outcomes are worse than disclosing upfront. Transparency at packaging stage builds trust that closes deals.

Module takeaway

4 weeks of documentation work can add 30-60% to your sale price by moving you from Grade B to Grade A on the AI Asset Score. The ROI is massive. Don't rush to list — package first, list when it's actually ready. Buyers pay for certainty.

Module 04

Writing a Listing That Gets Real Inquiries

"I posted on Indie Hackers. I posted on Twitter. I got questions from people with no money and no intention of buying. Zero serious inquiries. I don't know what I'm doing wrong."

Most AI project listings fail for one reason: they describe the product instead of the opportunity. Buyers don't want to understand your tech. They want to understand: can I make money with this, and is it safe?

The 5 elements of a listing that converts

  1. The hook (1 sentence): What does a buyer get? Not what the product does — what the buyer's life looks like after. Example: "A productized HR screening bot with 23 active clients and $2,800 MRR — runs without you, owner moving on."
  2. The math (3 numbers): Asking price, monthly net revenue, payback period. Always show the math. Never make the buyer calculate it themselves.
  3. The proof (2 pieces of evidence): Churn rate + trend, or customer count + retention. Something that shows this isn't going to collapse.
  4. The transfer story: What does the handover look like? "30-day transition support, full documentation, all credentials transfer, seller stays available for questions." Buyers pay for certainty.
  5. The buyer profile: Who is this right for? "Ideal for: a solo operator, a small agency, or a buyer with B2B SaaS background." Specificity attracts qualified buyers and filters time-wasters.

What to never put in a listing

🚩 Listing language that kills serious buyers
  • "Huge potential" — this means current revenue doesn't justify the price
  • "Easy to scale" — show the scaling path, don't just say it exists
  • "The right person could 10x this" — buyers price what exists, not what you imagine
  • Revenue without costs — show net, not gross
  • Vague terms like "significant," "strong," "loyal" — use numbers always

Where to list

Channels in order of quality for AI assets in $5k–$100k range:

  1. AIAsset.Market — vetted buyer base specifically looking for AI assets
  2. Indie Hackers — community of builders, some buyers (high noise, low conversion)
  3. MicroAcquire / Acquire.com — larger platform, more competition, lower signal below $50k
  4. Telegram communities — high engagement, buyers often move fast but may want to negotiate hard
  5. Twitter/X — good for awareness, not for closing — expect tire-kickers
Module takeaway

Your listing is marketing for a financial product. Write it like an investor memo, not a product launch post. Lead with the outcome for the buyer, support it with real numbers, and give enough detail that a serious buyer can evaluate without asking 20 questions first. The best listings attract 2-3 serious buyers, not 50 curious ones.
